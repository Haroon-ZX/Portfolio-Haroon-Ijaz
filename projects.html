AI-Powered Urban Co-Creation

Developing a Workshop Methodology for Future Visualisation with AI

Existing urban environment (Before) and AI-generated future visualisation (After).

During my research internship at Thomas More University of Applied Sciences, I developed and tested an AI-supported co-creation workshop methodology to help participants explore and visualise possible futures for urban public spaces. The work was carried out within the WATSUPS research context, focusing on Zandpoortvest in Mechelen, Belgium.

02 / MY ROLE

WATSUPS — Water as the Source of Urban Public Space

04 / LOCATION & PERIOD

Mechelen, Belgium · February–April 2026

02 / RESEARCH & DISCOVERY

Understanding the Urban Challenge

Understanding the Study Area

Zandpoortvest is an urban area in Mechelen, Belgium, close to the Thomas More University campus. It brings together educational facilities, transport infrastructure, public circulation routes and urban spaces used by different groups of people.

The area provided a practical context for exploring the relationship between urban infrastructure, everyday experience and the quality of public space. The research considered how a traffic-oriented environment, paved surfaces and the distribution of greenery and places to stay could influence the way people perceive and use an urban setting.

This study was situated within the WATSUPS research context — Water as the Source of Urban Public Space — which explores opportunities to reconsider urban public spaces through water, nature and more inclusive approaches to urban development.

Study area location — Zandpoortvest, Mechelen, near Thomas More University. The yellow circle indicates the selected research area.

Study Area — Zandpoortvest, Mechelen

The research focused on the Zandpoortvest area in Mechelen, Belgium, near the Thomas More University campus. The study area includes urban streets, educational facilities, pedestrian routes, traffic infrastructure and public spaces. This location provided the context for investigating existing urban conditions and exploring how participatory design and AI-supported visualisation could help people imagine possible future improvements.

Key Challenges Identified

Challenge 01 — Traffic-Dominated Streets

The study area includes major road infrastructure and significant vehicle circulation. In the documented street views, road space and traffic movement form prominent elements of the urban environment.

This raises questions about how urban spaces can balance mobility requirements with the needs of people who walk, cycle, gather or spend time outdoors. Within the research, traffic dominance became an important starting point for exploring alternative ways of imagining the public realm.

The photographs document visible traffic infrastructure; they do not independently establish traffic volumes, accident risks or measured pedestrian safety.

Challenge 02 — Limited Street Greenery

Although greenery is present in parts of the wider surroundings, the documented street-level environments show areas where planting and vegetation are limited compared with hard urban surfaces.

This observation encouraged exploration of how trees, planting and nature-based elements might be incorporated into future scenarios. It also connects to the broader WATSUPS interest in more nature-aware urban environments.

The observation concerns selected photographed locations rather than a measured assessment of greenery across the entire district.

Challenge 03 — Extensive Paved Surfaces

Several photographed areas contain large paved pedestrian and circulation spaces. These surfaces support movement and access, but they also offer opportunities to explore alternative spatial arrangements and additional environmental features.

The research considered how such spaces might be reimagined through different combinations of planting, social activity, water-sensitive features and public-space uses.

These were exploratory design questions rather than conclusions from a technical environmental assessment.

Challenge 04 — Limited Visible Public Seating

In selected photographs, dedicated seating opportunities are not clearly visible across some public areas. This observation raises questions about how spaces support staying, resting and informal social interaction, rather than only movement between destinations.

The research therefore considered how future visualisations could help participants discuss more welcoming public spaces and potential opportunities for social use.

The photographs alone cannot establish the total availability of seating or how frequently people use the area.

Beyond the Physical Environment: The Communication Challenge

Urban transformation is not only a question of identifying physical problems. It also involves helping different stakeholders communicate how they experience a place and what they would like to change.

People may have valuable ideas about greener streets, more comfortable public spaces or alternative urban experiences, but these ideas are not always easy to communicate through technical plans, written descriptions or conventional design language.

This communication challenge formed an important part of the internship assignment: exploring how generative AI could help participants translate their ideas into visual scenarios that others could see, question and discuss.

Rather than treating AI-generated images as final design proposals, the research approached them as conversation tools within a participatory process.

From Urban Challenges to Research Direction

These observations helped establish the practical context for developing an AI-supported co-creation workshop methodology.

The aim was not to produce a final urban redesign or claim that the identified challenges had been solved. Instead, the research explored how participants could move from understanding existing conditions to expressing possible future scenarios, supported by accessible digital tools and visual discussion.

This direction informed the next phase of the internship: designing a structured workshop process that connects participant observations, future ideas, AI prompt development, visual generation and collective reflection.

AI-GENERATED FUTURE VISION
Visualisation Outcome

Clearly communicate that this is an AI-generated workshop visualisation, not a constructed or approved urban design.

The AI-Ready Prompt — Full Version

Visualize a future transformation of this urban street into a human-centered, nature-inclusive environment connected to a nearby river. Replace large areas of hard, non-permeable surfaces with soft, natural materials such as permeable paths, wood, and textured stone designed for comfortable and accessible walking.

Remove polluting cars and buses, and redesign the street to prioritize pedestrians, bicycles, and small electric mini-buses. Ensure safe, inclusive movement for all users, including children, elderly people, and people with disabilities.

Create welcoming public spaces with seating areas, shaded zones, and social gathering spots that encourage people to meet, interact, and spend time outdoors.

Renovate building facades to be visually appealing and human-scaled, incorporating greenery such as green walls, balconies with plants, and natural materials.

Open up clear visual and physical connections to the river, ensuring the water is visible directly from the street through view corridors, lowered barriers, or aligned pathways. Design the street so the river becomes a central, visible feature of the experience.

Extend the design toward the river by creating a small urban beach with soft sand, native plants, and gentle access to the water, allowing people to relax, play, and engage with the riverside.

Include educational and interactive elements that help people learn about the river ecosystem, such as signage, observation decks, and nature-based play features.

Enhance ecological biodiversity by integrating trees, pollinator-friendly plants, and habitats for birds and insects, along with water-sensitive systems like rain gardens that connect to the river's natural cycle.

Create a balanced urban ecosystem where human comfort, safety, and social life coexist with thriving habitats for non-human species, supporting shade, shelter, food sources, and safe movement for all living beings.

05 / WORKSHOP FEEDBACK & KEY INSIGHTS

Workshop Feedback & Key Insights

After the co-creation workshop, feedback was collected to understand how the methodology could be improved. The reflections highlighted opportunities to strengthen collaboration, simplify activities and make AI-supported participation more accessible.

Original workshop feedback collected through the Miro board.

INSIGHT 01 — More Interaction Between Participants

Feedback highlighted the importance of allowing participants to talk to one another. Too much individual work can make co-creation feel isolated.

INSIGHT 02 — Combine Digital and Offline Activities

The feedback suggested combining AI tools with offline discussion and hands-on methods. This could help participants with different levels of digital confidence.

INSIGHT 03 — Simplify the Process

Suggestions included limiting the number of generated images, reducing the number of tasks and using a smaller selection of strong street photographs.

INSIGHT 04 — Better Time Management

Participants noted that working individually, generating images and discussing results could take considerable time. Clearer time guidance and more focused activities could improve the workshop experience.

The feedback showed that AI-supported visualisation has potential as a co-creation tool, but the quality of the experience also depends on facilitation, accessibility, time management and opportunities for meaningful interaction.

SECTION 03 / RESEARCH INSIGHTS

What We Discovered

Through hospital observations, staff interviews, spatial mapping, and analysis of existing communication touchpoints, our team identified four interconnected barriers affecting how patient rights were communicated and understood within the elderly division at OPZ Geel.

FOUR KEY PAIN POINTS
01
PATIENT PERSPECTIVE

Lack of Visibility

Important information about patient rights was not consistently visible throughout the care environment. Patients could receive information during admission but might not encounter clear reminders during their ongoing treatment.

Patient-rights communication needs to remain visible beyond the first interaction.

02
PATIENT PERSPECTIVE

Too Complex

Existing patient-rights information could be difficult to understand because of formal language, lengthy explanations, and information-heavy communication materials. This created additional barriers for people who needed straightforward guidance.

Accessible language and visual communication can make information easier to understand.

03
PATIENT PERSPECTIVE

Not One Place for Information

Information was distributed across different communication touchpoints. Without one recognizable reference point, patients could struggle to locate the information they needed at the right moment.

A clearly identifiable information point can support orientation and independent access.

04
CAREGIVER PERSPECTIVE

Lack of a System

Caregivers played an important role in explaining patient rights, but communication relied heavily on individual verbal explanations. A more consistent approach and practical support materials were needed.

Staff need shared tools and a recognizable communication system to reinforce information consistently.

01 / PROJECT OVERVIEW

Reimagining Recreation Beyond Summer

Service Design & Visitor Experience

Summer recreation — an active lakeside destination

Zilvermeer is a recreational destination in Mol, Belgium, offering a combination of nature, water activities, hospitality and leisure facilities. Its summer environment is active and vibrant, but seasonal changes raise questions about how the destination can remain welcoming and engaging throughout the year.

Our project explored the relationship between Zilvermeer's visitor experience, organisational structure and stakeholder network, with a particular interest in opportunities for off-season activation.

Our research revealed that improving the IJsvogel experience required more than introducing new activities. The existing service environment presented challenges related to navigation, ordering, circulation, space organisation and seasonal use.

By examining these issues, we identified opportunities to connect operational improvements with a broader strategy for activating the destination beyond the summer season.

Existing Service Challenges

01
Confusing Ordering and Pickup

At the snack bar, visitors could experience uncertainty about where to order, where to wait and where to collect their food.

The existing service arrangement created opportunities for overlapping queues and interruptions to visitor circulation.

Design opportunity Establish clearer ordering, waiting and collection zones, supported by visible information and intuitive circulation.
02
Wayfinding and Visibility

Visitors moving between the entrance, snack bar, cocktail bar, terrace and other facilities could benefit from clearer directional information.

Busy service areas and inconsistent signage made orientation an important part of the visitor experience.

Design opportunity Improve the visibility and consistency of navigation, including entrances, service counters and connections between indoor and outdoor spaces.
03
Underused Off-season Potential

IJsvogel's existing facilities were strongly associated with summer recreation, while the building and its surrounding environment offered opportunities for other types of group experiences.

06 / EXPERIENCE DESIGN

Connecting the Experience From First Contact to Final Goodbye

A successful group experience depends on more than the activities offered on site.

The booking process, preparation, arrival, wayfinding, staff coordination, hospitality and follow-up all contribute to the overall visitor experience.

Our team explored how these touchpoints could work together as one connected service.

07 / OUTCOME & REFLECTION

From Research to a Shared Vision

Bringing the Concept Together

The project brought together stakeholder research, site observations, user personas, spatial concept exploration and service design to propose a more flexible future for IJsvogel.

Rather than focusing on a single intervention, our team developed a connected experience direction that considered how spaces, hospitality, activities and operational needs could support different visitor groups during quieter seasons.

The final work was communicated through research materials, spatial visualisations, experience planning, a service blueprint, a digital project website and a conceptual 3D walkthrough.

These outputs represent academic design proposals and explorations rather than implemented or commercially evaluated solutions.

My Role in the Project

Stakeholder Research · Service Strategy · Experience Design

My role in the Zilvermeer project focused on stakeholder research, service strategy, and developing opportunities for year-round visitor experiences.

I explored how Zilvermeer operates as a recreational destination, considering the relationships between its management, hospitality partners, staff, and visitors. Through site observations, research, and team discussions, I helped identify opportunities to make better use of existing facilities during the quieter seasons.

During the IJsvogel concept development, I contributed to exploring how the building could function as a flexible hub for corporate gatherings, sports communities, team-building activities, and social events.

This included developing service ideas around space zoning, group activities, food and beverage services, and event packages that connect indoor facilities with the surrounding natural environment.

Working with my teammates, I helped translate research findings into practical service opportunities and a more connected visitor experience.

What This Project Taught Me

This project helped me understand that service design is not only about creating new experiences. It is also about recognising the potential of what already exists.

Zilvermeer had valuable spaces, natural surroundings, and established services. The challenge was to explore how these different elements could work together more effectively beyond the summer season.

One of my biggest learnings was the importance of considering different perspectives. Visitors want enjoyable and accessible experiences, while staff, management, and hospitality partners must consider operational realities.

Coming from a background in AI and Computer Science, this project strengthened my ability to approach complex challenges through human-centred research, stakeholder collaboration, and service design.

"Good design connects people, places, and services to create experiences that are meaningful for visitors and realistic for the people delivering them."
